\begin{abstract}
Independent machine-level forecasts of cloud telemetry often violate exact
machine-to-cluster conservation, producing incoherent capacity views even when
point accuracy appears acceptable. Using the public TimeTrack OpenAirInterface
({OAI}) continuous integration/continuous deployment telemetry dataset, we
evaluate hierarchy-aware forecasting on verified physical hierarchies for
core-weighted {CPU}, exact memory aggregation, and disk-level summing.
Baselines include persistence, exponentially weighted moving average ({EWMA}),
and Ridge regression; learned models include LightGBM and DLinear; reconciliation
modes include independent forecasts, bottom-up, weighted least squares ({WLS}),
and MinT-shrink, with top-down used for the disk boundary.
On {CPU}, LightGBM with MinT yields the best observed frozen outer-evaluation
one-step accuracy while restoring exact coherence.
On memory, reconciliation often improves DLinear relative to its own independent
forecast, yet {EWMA} remains the strongest observed method.
On disk, Ridge bottom-up degrades aggregate accuracy, whereas top-down preserves
the independent top forecast at a bottom-level cost.
Ordinary aggregate mean absolute error ({MAE}) gains do not transfer to universal
peak-operational improvements.
These results support selective, hierarchy-dependent reconciliation as a practical
layer for coherent multi-level telemetry forecasting in application-profiling
pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Cloud--edge and cloud-native platforms generate telemetry at several physical
and administrative levels at once: containers or workloads, machines or nodes,
clusters, and, in continuum settings, higher-level orchestration views
\cite{buyya2019manifesto,shi2016edge,satyanarayanan2017emergence}.
Capacity allocation, proactive scaling, placement, admission control,
service-level agreement ({SLA}) risk assessment, and ongoing profile maintenance
increasingly depend on forecasts of those signals, not only on instantaneous
measurements
\cite{lorido2014review,qu2018auto}.
When decisions consume both machine-level and cluster-level forecasts, the
forecasts must describe the same underlying system rather than two
independently convenient summaries of related but unconstrained streams.

Independently accurate forecasts can still fail in operation.
Consider seven machine {CPU} forecasts whose core-weighted combination implies
one cluster total, while a separately trained cluster forecast implies another.
Each series may achieve a competitive mean absolute error ({MAE}), yet the two
totals cannot simultaneously represent the same physical hierarchy.
The resulting inconsistency can produce contradictory capacity estimates,
incoherent application profiles, unstable or infeasible orchestration actions,
and difficulty explaining forecast-driven decisions, including double counting
or underestimating available resources.
In short, point-wise competitiveness does not guarantee a usable joint plan.
Forecast coherence---exact consistency with verified machine-to-cluster
aggregation---is therefore an operational requirement for multi-level planning,
not merely a statistical preference
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical}.

Conventional cloud workload and resource forecasting largely optimizes point
accuracy on monitored streams
\cite{calheiros2015workload,masdari2020workload,khan2022workload}.
Autoscaling and proactive resource-management studies likewise often treat host
and aggregate telemetry as independent inputs to control loops
\cite{toka2021kubernetes,casalicchio2019container}.
Model comparisons typically ask which predictor minimizes error, whereas fewer
evaluations ask whether forecasts respect exact multi-level identities that
link machines to clusters.
Selecting the lowest-{MAE} model therefore does not automatically resolve
machine-to-cluster inconsistency: an accurate independent bottom forecast and
an accurate independent top forecast may still disagree about the same physical
quantity.

This paper continues a line of application-profiling research in the
cloud--edge continuum.
Unified application-profile models for microservices-based applications
formalize requirements, components, deployment constraints, and continuum
resources in a single representation, and identify predictive resource
behavior as a direction for moving from descriptive profiles toward
proactive profile management.
The present work develops and evaluates the coherent forecasting and
reconciliation layer needed for that progression.
It does not redesign a profile ontology or schema; instead, it supplies
freeze-scoped forecast evidence that can later populate or maintain dynamic
application-profile fields under exact machine-to-cluster constraints.

We study the problem on TimeTrack, a public OpenAirInterface ({OAI}) continuous
integration/continuous deployment ({CI}/{CD}) telemetry dataset with seven
machines, cluster-level aggregates, and multiple resource metrics
\cite{meliani2025timetrack,meliani2025timetrackkaggle}.
In our pipeline the measured median sampling interval is approximately
$42.285$\,s.
The hierarchies used here have exact or otherwise verified aggregation
relations and exhibit operationally realistic {CI}/{CD} workload variation.
We distinguish roles carefully: the TimeTrack dataset paper presents and
characterizes the corpus; the Kaggle release provides public access; Zero-Touch
Predictive Orchestration studies automated model generation and cold-start
mitigation on related continuum forecasting problems
\cite{meliani2026zerotouch};
and the present paper evaluates hierarchy-aware coherence, reconciliation
trade-offs, stochastic robustness, and failure boundaries on frozen TimeTrack
artifacts.
Dataset creation is not claimed as a contribution here.

The research gap is the lack of an evaluation that jointly combines verified
physical machine-to-cluster telemetry hierarchies; strong deterministic and
learned forecasting baselines; independent forecasts together with multiple
reconciliation strategies; leakage-controlled chronological outer evaluation;
moving-block bootstrap with multiple-comparison correction; stochastic-seed
robustness; top-versus-bottom accuracy trade-offs; peak-operational
qualification; and both positive and negative hierarchy cases.
General hierarchical forecasting and reconciliation methods exist
\cite{wickramasuriya2019mint,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical},
and related cloud hierarchical learning appears in settings such as
end-to-end multi-indicator models
\cite{harmony2024},
yet their behavior is not automatically transferable to cloud telemetry.
Hierarchy semantics differ by metric; top and bottom predictability differ;
disk levels can include resets or large downward movements; reconciliation can
propagate poor bottom forecasts into aggregates; and operational peak metrics
may respond differently from ordinary average error.
An evidence-based account of when reconciliation helps, when it is conditional,
and when it fails is therefore required before coherent forecasts can be treated
as a default planning layer.

Our study therefore compares persistence, exponentially weighted moving average
({EWMA}), Ridge regression, LightGBM
\cite{ke2017lightgbm},
and DLinear
\cite{zeng2023dlinear}
under independent, bottom-up, weighted least squares ({WLS}), and MinT-shrink
modes, with top-down reserved for the disk boundary.
Hierarchies comprise verified core-weighted {CPU}, an exact memory sum, and an
exact disk-level sum.
Evaluation uses chronological outer folds, multiple multi-output-width
configurations, train-only
hyperparameter selection, seeds $0/1/2$ for LightGBM and DLinear, and immutable
frozen evidence layers culminating in the reporting freeze.
Detailed formulations and protocol parameters are given in
Sections~\ref{sec:methods} and~\ref{sec:protocol}; the Introduction states only
the design commitments needed to interpret the research questions and claims.

In the frozen outer evaluation, LightGBM independent outperforms Ridge by about
$17.3\%$ on core-weighted {CPU}, and LightGBM MinT further reduces aggregate
{CPU} {MAE} by about $10.1\%$ relative to LightGBM independent while restoring
exact coherence; LightGBM is seed-invariant under the frozen configuration, and
DLinear reconciliation gains remain stable across seeds.
On memory, reconciliation often improves DLinear relative to its own independent
forecast, yet {EWMA} remains the strongest robust method and reconciled DLinear
does not robustly outperform {EWMA}.
On disk, Ridge bottom-up harms aggregate accuracy, whereas top-down preserves
the independent top forecast at a bottom-level cost.
Across peak and high-load diagnostics, ordinary aggregate-{MAE} gains do not
imply universal peak-recall or false-alarm improvements.
These findings are scoped to the frozen TimeTrack evaluation and are presented
as observed outer-evaluation outcomes rather than as a prospectively selected
deployment policy.

These observations motivate five research questions.
\textbf{RQ1:} How do independent and reconciled forecasts affect aggregate
accuracy and exact machine-to-cluster coherence for core-weighted {CPU}?
\textbf{RQ2:} Do the {CPU} findings remain stable across folds, multi-output-width
configurations, models, and stochastic seeds?
\textbf{RQ3:} When does reconciliation improve memory forecasts, and how do
reconciled learned models compare with {EWMA}?
\textbf{RQ4:} Why can reconciliation degrade disk-level forecasts, and what
trade-offs arise between bottom-up and top-down modes?
\textbf{RQ5:} Do ordinary aggregate-accuracy gains transfer to high-load and
peak-operational metrics?

Using only frozen final evidence, this paper contributes:
(i)~a hierarchy-aware forecasting formulation for verified machine-to-cluster
{CPU}, memory, and disk relations, enabling exact coherence assessment rather
than independent stream scoring alone;
(ii)~a leakage-controlled comparison of persistence, {EWMA}, Ridge, LightGBM,
and DLinear under independent, bottom-up, {WLS}, and MinT-shrink modes, with
top-down for disk, quantifying accuracy--coherence and top/bottom trade-offs;
(iii)~seed-robust {CPU} evidence that LightGBM+MinT is the best observed frozen
outer-evaluation configuration for aggregate accuracy with restored coherence;
(iv)~conditional memory evidence that reconciled DLinear can improve over its
independent forecast while {EWMA} remains the strongest robust method;
(v)~a disk failure boundary in which Ridge bottom-up harms aggregates and
top-down preserves the independent top at bottom cost; and
(vi)~peak-operational qualification showing that ordinary aggregate-{MAE} gains
do not imply universal high-load or peak improvements.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work;
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes TimeTrack and the hierarchies;
Sections~\ref{sec:methods}--\ref{sec:protocol} present methodology and protocol;
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations; and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Application Profiling in the Cloud--Edge Continuum}
Application-profile and lifecycle-management research in the cloud--edge
continuum seeks declarative descriptions that make placement, adaptation, and
operations tractable across heterogeneous tiers
\cite{duc2020survey,taherizadeh2018monitoring,buyya2019manifesto}.
Typical profiles encode components and dependencies, resource requirements,
deployment constraints, placement preferences, service objectives, and lifecycle
hooks that orchestration platforms can consume.
Container orchestration surveys likewise emphasize monitoring and control loops
that operate on multi-granularity telemetry rather than on a single aggregate
view
\cite{casalicchio2019container,rossi2020geo,toka2021kubernetes}.
As continuum applications span edge and cloud nodes, those loops increasingly
need forecasts that remain meaningful when read jointly at machine and cluster
scopes.

Telemetry-aware and adaptive profiling extends these representations by feeding
current measurements---and, increasingly, predicted resource behavior---into
placement and scaling decisions
\cite{taherizadeh2018monitoring,duc2020survey}.
Consistency becomes harder when predictions are produced independently at
machine and cluster levels: a profile that stores both views can encode
contradictory capacity expectations even when each forecast appears accurate in
isolation.
In contrast to representation-centric profile work, forecast-oriented cloud
management literature typically optimizes prediction error or scaling cost
without asking whether multi-level profile fields remain jointly coherent.

Unified application-profile models for microservices in the continuum
formalize requirements, components, constraints, and resources in one schema
and identify predictive resource behavior as a step toward proactive profile
maintenance.
The present paper supplies a forecasting and reconciliation evaluation that may
support such future maintenance under exact machine-to-cluster identities.
It evaluates coherent telemetry forecasts; it does not propose another
application-profile schema or claim an end-to-end profile-management system.

\subsection{Cloud Telemetry and Workload Forecasting}
Classical cloud resource forecasting established persistence, smoothing, and
autoregressive baselines as practical references for provisioning and quality of
service
\cite{calheiros2015workload,islam2012empirical}.
Persistence and exponentially weighted moving average ({EWMA}) remain important
not because they are sophisticated, but because they expose whether learned
models improve upon stable, train-only-tuned alternatives under the same
protocol.
Without such references, apparent gains from tree ensembles or neural forecasters
can be overstated relative to inexpensive, reproducible baselines that operators
already understand.
Surveys of workload and resource forecasting therefore treat strong baselines as
part of evaluation hygiene rather than as obsolete methods
\cite{masdari2020workload,lorido2014review}.

Machine-learning approaches, including regression, tree ensembles such as
LightGBM
\cite{ke2017lightgbm},
and neural models, are widely applied to {CPU}, memory, request rates, and
related autoscaling signals
\cite{khan2022workload,qu2018auto,toka2021kubernetes}.
Their common evaluation practice optimizes point accuracy on independently
monitored streams and then, in operational settings, feeds those forecasts into
reactive or proactive controllers.
This line of work addresses model selection and control policy design rather
than hierarchy consistency: host and cluster signals may both be predicted, yet
nothing enforces that the predictions describe one physical system.

Deep forecasting surveys document rapid progress with recurrent, attention, and
Transformer architectures
\cite{lim2021temporal,wen2023transformers}.
Comparative evidence nevertheless cautions that complex models are not
automatically better across datasets and metrics
\cite{makridakis2018statistical,zeng2023dlinear}.
DLinear is particularly relevant here as a deliberately simple neural baseline
that can match or exceed more elaborate Transformers on several forecasting
benchmarks
\cite{zeng2023dlinear}.
Using LightGBM and DLinear in our study therefore tests competitive learned
forecasters; it does not claim that the models themselves are novel.

Cloud-native and edge platforms consume node and cluster telemetry for
Kubernetes scaling, geographically distributed deployment, and continuum
orchestration
\cite{toka2021kubernetes,rossi2020geo,casalicchio2019container}.
Proactive autoscaling taxonomies stress bursty demand and the cost of delayed
reactions
\cite{lorido2014review,qu2018auto},
yet controllers typically treat monitored streams as independent inputs.
Signature-based and anomaly-aware predictors further show that average error can
hide costly deviations in resource usage
\cite{sus2024signature}.
Spike-sensitive adjustments for cloud provisioning likewise argue that ordinary
forecast scores understate sudden increases that drive under-provisioning
\cite{agullo2025spikes}.
These perspectives motivate peak and high-load qualification ({RQ5}) without
turning this manuscript into a full autoscaling or {SLA}-control study.

Public multi-metric telecommunications cloud-native traces remain comparatively
scarce.
TimeTrack provides {OAI} {CI}/{CD} telemetry with machine and cluster views
\cite{meliani2025timetrack,meliani2025timetrackkaggle}.
The dataset paper characterizes the corpus; the Kaggle release enables public
reuse; Zero-Touch Predictive Orchestration studies automated model generation
and cold-start mitigation on related continuum forecasting problems
\cite{meliani2026zerotouch}.
Unlike those contributions, the present study does not automate model search or
reintroduce the dataset.
It instead evaluates machine-to-cluster coherence under classical bottom-up,
{WLS}, and MinT selectivity, including disk failure boundaries, multi-seed
robustness, and peak qualification on frozen TimeTrack hierarchies.

\subsection{Hierarchical Forecasting and Reconciliation}
Independently forecast nodes of a hierarchy generally violate aggregation
constraints: base forecasts need not coincide with coherent forecasts that
respect a summing matrix linking bottom series to aggregates
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,hyndman2021forecasting}.
The forecasting literature therefore separates the production of base forecasts
from their projection onto the constraint space, treating coherence as a
structural requirement rather than as an optional post-check.
We leave formal matrix notation to Section~\ref{sec:methods} and focus here on
method classes and their empirical assumptions.

Classical strategies include bottom-up aggregation of bottom forecasts,
top-down disaggregation of a top forecast, ordinary least-squares combination,
and weighted least squares ({WLS})
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical}.
Bottom-up preserves bottoms but can inject bottom-level error into the aggregate;
top-down preserves the top while redistributing residuals to bottoms; combination
methods adjust all levels using estimated error structure.
The practical choice among these modes therefore depends on where forecast error
is concentrated and on whether the operational objective privileges top accuracy,
bottom fidelity, or joint coherence.
Where bottom predictability is weak, bottom-up can therefore harm aggregates---a
failure mode that is especially relevant for disk-like telemetry with resets or
large downward movements.

MinT reconciles base forecasts by minimizing the trace of the reconciled error
covariance under the summing constraints, often with shrinkage estimators that
stabilize covariance estimates in moderate samples
\cite{wickramasuriya2019mint,schafer2005shrinkage}.
Geometric interpretations clarify bias and projection properties of
reconciliation
\cite{panagiotelis2021geometric},
while linear-model formulations show that forecasting and reconciliation can be
combined efficiently when base models are linear
\cite{ashouri2022fast}.
Recent reviews synthesize these developments across point and probabilistic
settings
\cite{athanasopoulos2024hierarchical,panagiotelis2023probabilistic}.
The present paper reuses classical MinT-shrink/{WLS}/bottom-up/top-down
machinery; it does not introduce a new reconciliation algorithm.

Computing systems increasingly explore hierarchical or multi-level resource
prediction, but often under different hierarchy semantics.
{HARMONY} couples hierarchical multi-indicator attention forecasting with
{Bayesian} scaling decisions for cloud resources; its hierarchy is
indicator-structural and end-to-end rather than a post-hoc classical
reconciliation suite on exact machine-to-cluster sums
\cite{harmony2024}.
The Flow-based Reconcile Transformer ({FRT}) jointly forecasts and reconciles
hierarchical series with coherent deep generative modeling and reports
data-center workload deployments, again as an end-to-end neural method rather
than a selective classical ablation on physical {CPU}, memory, and disk
identities
\cite{wang2025frt}.
{MaMiClif} models macro-micro microservice indicator dependencies in logical
data centers through collaborative graph learning; the hierarchy is
service/indicator relational rather than an exact machine-to-cluster summing
relation
\cite{gao2026mamiclif}.
These systems are valuable adjacent baselines for hierarchical cloud prediction
and coherent deep forecasting.
Across them, however, a method that improves aggregate prediction for one
hierarchy definition need not transfer to core-weighted {CPU}, additive memory,
or disk levels whose bottom errors can propagate under bottom-up reconciliation.
That transfer question is empirical and hierarchy-dependent, which is precisely
the scoped evaluation gap addressed below.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Table~\ref{tab:related} situates the closest work.
Cloud forecasting usually evaluates independent streams; reconciliation research
often uses economic, energy, retail, tourism, or synthetic hierarchies; and
computing-oriented hierarchical studies may omit strong deterministic baselines,
seed robustness, or negative hierarchy cases
\cite{masdari2020workload,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical,harmony2024,wang2025frt}.
Average forecasting metrics also may not describe peak-operational behavior
relevant to provisioning risk
\cite{agullo2025spikes,sus2024signature}.
Few evaluated systems combine verified physical machine-to-cluster relations,
metric-specific hierarchy semantics, classical reconciliation selectivity, and
operational peak qualification on public {OAI} telemetry.

Relative to those closest studies, this work jointly examines verified
core-weighted {CPU}, exact memory, and exact disk hierarchies on TimeTrack.
It compares persistence, {EWMA}, Ridge, LightGBM, and DLinear under independent,
bottom-up, {WLS}, and MinT-shrink modes, with top-down reserved for the disk
boundary, and reports leakage-controlled outer folds, moving-block bootstrap
with Holm correction, seeds $0/1/2$, top/bottom trade-offs, peak qualification,
and a disk negative boundary.
That scoped combination addresses {RQ1}--{RQ5} without claiming a universal first
method or cross-dataset numerical superiority.

\section{TimeTrack Dataset and Telemetry Hierarchies}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Collection environment and trace structure.}
TimeTrack is a public high-resolution telemetry corpus collected from an
operational OpenAirInterface ({OAI}) continuous integration and deployment
({CI}/{CD}) cluster that supports development and testing of {OAI} components
such as the {gNB}, user equipment ({UE}), and core-network software
\cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
The published environment comprises seven physical machines with $236$ {CPU}
cores, $437.5$\,{GB} {RAM}, approximately $1.8$\,{TB} of {SSD} storage, and
multiple physical network interfaces; Prometheus is reported as the underlying
metric source \cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
Released traces cover computing, per-core {CPU}, disk, memory, network, and
latency signals and are available on {Kaggle}
\cite{meliani2025timetrackkaggle}.
Dataset creation is not a contribution of this manuscript: we take the released
files as given and begin with hierarchy validation and hierarchy-aware
forecasting on the metrics required for the accepted exact {CPU}, memory, and
disk relations summarized in Table~\ref{tab:hierarchies}.
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows independent bottom- and top-level forecasts under
exact summing constraints with optional reconciliation.

\paragraph{Timeline preparation and integrity checks.}
The dataset paper describes a nominal collection interval of approximately
$45$\,s \cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
Direct timestamp analysis yields a measured median interval of approximately
$42.285$\,s (about $99.93\%$ of consecutive steps round to $42$\,s), which we
use for wall-clock interpretation of the sampling step.
Five primary traces share $41{,}362$ timestamps from
$2024$-$06$-$24$~$13$:$37$:$06$ to $2024$-$07$-$19$~$16$:$27$:$05$
($\approx 25.12$ calendar days), with no duplicate timestamps and strictly
increasing order.
A major outage from $2024$-$06$-$28$~$13$:$10$:$49$ to
$2024$-$07$-$03$~$10$:$05$:$20$ ($\approx 4.87$ days) is not interpolated:
multi-day fill would invent temporal structure.
Forecasting windows use only the retained continuous post-outage segment
($33{,}235$ observations); no context window may cross the outage.
Exact fold boundaries are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
Sparse missing cells outside the outage are left missing rather than filled
across the break; hierarchy identity checks use timestamp-aligned rows without
imputing invented values.

\paragraph{Selected metrics.}
We use per-machine {CPU} consumption percentages with a core-weighted cluster
representation; used-memory levels (bytes) at machine and cluster scope; and
used-disk levels ({UD}, bytes) at both scopes.
{UD} denotes a used-disk level, not disk write rate, disk read rate, or
cumulative {I}/{O} counters.
Network-interface and latency telemetry are present in TimeTrack but are not
used for a final network-hierarchy claim: candidate bond/interface aggregation
did not meet the same exact physical-identity bar as {CPU}, memory, and disk.

\paragraph{Core-weighted {CPU} hierarchy.}
Let $u_{i,t}$ be machine~$i$'s {CPU}-utilization percentage and $c_i$ its
verified core count, with $C=\sum_i c_i=236$.
Reported cluster {CPU} is the weighted mean $(\sum_i c_i u_{i,t})/C$.
Reconciliation uses the additive weighted-sum hierarchy
$y^{\mathrm{CPU}}_t=\sum_i c_i u_{i,t}$, converting to percentage only for
reporting as $y^{\mathrm{CPU}}_t/236$.
An unweighted mean would give equal weight to machines with unequal capacity.
Verified counts are
$(c_i)=(36,48,36,36,20,36,24)$ for machines~$01$--$07$ (total~$236$).
The TimeTrack resource table and raw static core-count fields swap the unequal
machine05/machine07 labels relative to the released per-core host identifiers
\cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
We resolve this by data-derived validation---correlating machine-level {CU}
with per-core host traces, comparing candidate mappings to host column
inventories, and retaining the mapping that yields an exact weighted-sum
identity with $C=236$---not by arbitrary relabeling.
The total remains $236$, but the two unequal machine-specific labels are
exchanged between the publication table and the telemetry identifiers; we use
the empirically verified mapping throughout forecasting and reconciliation.

\paragraph{Memory and disk hierarchies.}
Used memory obeys an exact byte-sum identity: the cluster used-memory series
equals the sum of the seven machine used-memory series.
Bytes are already additive, so no capacity weighting is needed.
On all $41{,}362$ timestamp-aligned compute rows the maximum absolute identity
error is~$0$ (no imputation for this check).
Independent forecasts can still violate the identity; reconciliation restores
it on forecasts.
Used disk obeys the analogous exact level-sum identity on the same
$41{,}362$ aligned rows, again with maximum absolute identity error~$0$.
{UD} is a level series: downward jumps may reflect cleanup, deletion, rotation,
or reset behavior, unlike smoother capacity-like dynamics.
Exact structural coherence does not imply that every reconciliation method
improves accuracy when bottoms respond differently to downward changes;
measured trade-offs are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Scope of the final hierarchy study.}
Before forecasting we verified numerically that core-weighted {CPU} is exact
under the verified mapping, that used-memory and used-disk sums are exact on
aligned observations, and that summing matrices encode these physical relations
for later reconciliation of forecasts.
Approximate network bond/interface candidates were inspected and excluded from
final claims.
This manuscript's contribution therefore begins after dataset release, with
validated hierarchies and the frozen protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\section{Forecasting and Reconciliation Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Forecasting Problem and Hierarchical Representation}
\label{sec:forecast-problem}
Let $n=7$ denote the machine-level bottom series and $m=n+1=8$ the full
hierarchy.
Write bottom observations $\mathbf{b}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and the stacked
bottom-plus-top vector $\mathbf{y}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$.
With summing matrix $\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}_t=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}_t.
\label{eq:hierarchy}
\end{equation}
The bottom block of $\mathbf{S}$ is the identity; its top row encodes
core-weighted contributions for {CPU} and direct additive values for memory and
disk (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}).
For {CPU}, reconciliation uses weighted-sum units and totals are divided by
$236$ only when reporting weighted-mean percentages.

Given origin $t$, context length $L$, and joint prediction width $H$, each series
maps the lagged window ending at $t$ to a length-$H$ target vector.
Independent base forecasts are written $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t+H\mid t}$.
In the frozen pipeline, $L=32$, inputs are univariate lagged targets (no
calendar covariates), and each bottom and top series is trained separately.
Windows with missing values or outage crossings are discarded; identical
origins are retained across levels so reconciliation acts on aligned forecasts.
Fold construction is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
Here $H\in\{1,8,16\}$ (disk $H\in\{1,8\}$) denotes the number of future samples
jointly predicted during fitting; scored lead and fold execution are specified
in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

Observed telemetry may satisfy~\eqref{eq:hierarchy} exactly while independent
forecasts do not.
Coherence requires
$\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t+H\mid t}=\mathbf{S}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t+H\mid t}$,
equivalently $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{t+H\mid t}\in\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{S})$.
The objective is to evaluate three properties that can conflict: top-level
accuracy, bottom-level accuracy, and exact top--bottom coherence.

\paragraph{Persistence and {EWMA}.}
Persistence forecasts the last context observation,
$\hat{y}_{t+1\mid t}=y_t$, tiled across the joint prediction width $H$.
It is a demanding baseline for slowly varying memory and disk levels and for
one-step evaluation.
{EWMA} recurses over the context,
$z_{\ell}=\alpha y_{t-L+1+\ell}+(1-\alpha)z_{\ell-1}$, initialized at the first
sample, and forecasts $\hat{y}_{t+1\mid t}=z_{L-1}$ for every output component.
Alpha selection and the frozen accepted value are specified in
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
{EWMA} is deterministic, inexpensive, and responsive to recent observations.

\paragraph{Ridge and LightGBM.}
Ridge is $L_2$-penalized lag regression,
$\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\mathbf{z}\|_2^2
+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$,
fit per multi-output component on flattened lags as a regularized linear
benchmark.
LightGBM fits gradient-boosted trees on the same features, with a separate model
per series, fold, and joint prediction width \cite{ke2017lightgbm}.
Family-specific frozen hyperparameters, disk transfer, and seed-invariance
behavior are specified in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\paragraph{DLinear.}
DLinear is a lightweight direct neural baseline, not a Transformer
\cite{zeng2023dlinear}.
It splits the univariate context into moving-average trend and residual seasonal
components (kernel size~$25$) and applies separate linear maps from length~$L$
to length~$H$.
Only the target series enters the network.
Train-only standardization is inverse-transformed before metrics; the frozen
training budget is given in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
DLinear is seed-sensitive under that budget and is evaluated as a lightweight
neural competitor rather than a deep sequence model.
\label{sec:reconciliation}
Write independent base forecasts
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}=(\hat{\mathbf{b}}^\top,\hat{y}_{\mathrm{top}})^\top$.
Linear reconciliation takes the form
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{G}\hat{\mathbf{y}},
\label{eq:recon}
\end{equation}
so $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\in\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{S})$ by construction
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical}.
Independent forecasting is the unreconciled reference---separate top and bottom
forecasts with no projection---and is not itself a reconciliation method.

Bottom-up sets $\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ and
$\tilde{y}_{\mathrm{top}}=\mathbf{1}^\top\hat{\mathbf{b}}$, i.e.\
$\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{BU}}=\mathbf{S}\hat{\mathbf{b}}$.
Bottoms are unchanged, so bottom accuracy is preserved while bottom bias
propagates to the top.
For disk only, top-down uses
$p_i=\hat{b}_i/(\sum_j\hat{b}_j+\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon=10^{-12}$ and
$\tilde{b}_i^{\mathrm{TD}}=p_i\hat{y}_{\mathrm{top}}$, preserving the independent
top exactly while redistributing bottoms.

Projection methods use
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
&=
\mathbf{S}
\bigl(\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\bigr)^{+}
\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{-1}
\hat{\mathbf{y}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
with Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse $(\cdot)^{+}$
\cite{wickramasuriya2019mint}.
{OLS} ($\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}$) is retained only as an ablation at $H=8$.
{WLS} uses diagonal residual variances from outer-train/inner-validation
residuals (floored at $10^{-12}$).
MinT-shrink uses a residual covariance diagonally shrunk at intensity $0.1$,
with an additional $0.1$ shrink and small ridge in the reconciler for numerical
stability \cite{schafer2005shrinkage,wickramasuriya2019mint}.
These estimators are variance-optimal under stated second-moment assumptions;
they do not guarantee lower realized {MAE}.
Outer-evaluation residuals are never used to form $\mathbf{W}$
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

The coherence residual is the mean absolute
$|\hat{y}_{\mathrm{top}}-\mathbf{s}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{b}}|$
({CPU} in weighted-sum units).
Reconciled forecasts should be zero within floating-point tolerance, but
coherence alone does not imply accuracy.
Mean reconciliation adjustment is the mean absolute difference between
reconciled and independent forecasts over all hierarchy series.

\subsection{Accuracy, Coherence, and Operational Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Primary accuracy is
$\mathrm{MAE}=N^{-1}\sum_t|y_t-\hat{y}_t|$,
kept in the target's physical unit \cite{hyndman2006accuracy}.
{RMSE} emphasizes large errors;
{MASE} scales {MAE} by the training lag-$1$ naive mean absolute difference and
is marked undefined (never replaced by $0$ or $\infty$) when that scale is below
$10^{-12}$ or fewer than two finite pairs exist;
$R^2$ is supporting only.
Errors are never pooled across {CPU} percentages, memory bytes, and disk levels.
We report top {MAE}, bottom macro {MAE}, core-weighted bottom {MAE} for {CPU},
and worst-machine {MAE}.
Hierarchy core weighting is distinct from macro averaging of machine errors: a
top gain can conceal machine-level degradation.

Descriptive relative change uses
\begin{equation*}
\Delta_{\mathrm{rel}}
=
(\mathrm{MAE}_A-\mathrm{MAE}_B)/\mathrm{MAE}_B,
\end{equation*}
with negative values favoring method~$A$.
Bootstrap relative effects, peak-threshold transfer, and related protocol
settings are specified in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}; numerical outcomes appear
in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Chronological Evaluation Design}
Claim-generating runs use the retained continuous post-outage timeline.
Train, inner-validation, and outer-test partitions preserve temporal order; no
random shuffling is used for fold construction
\cite{bergmeir2018note}.
Three expanding chronological outer folds partition the post-outage span into an
initial training seed plus successive test blocks.
For fold $k$, timestamps before the test block form the outer-train span; the
last $15\%$ of that span is inner validation; the remainder is training; the test
block stays untouched until final scoring.

Context length is $L=32$
($\approx 32\times 42.285\,\mathrm{s}\approx 22.6$\,min at the median interval),
stated only as an approximate elapsed-time reading because timestamps need not be
perfectly regular.
Windows that would cross the multi-day outage, a fold boundary, or a
missing-value discontinuity are discarded, and identical origins are retained
across hierarchy levels for aligned reconciliation.

Joint prediction widths $H\in\{1,8,16\}$ ({CPU}/memory; disk $H\in\{1,8\}$)
are the number of future samples predicted jointly during fitting.
The target vector is $(y_{t+1},\ldots,y_{t+H})$; frozen evaluation extracts only
component~$0$, so every reported point, reconciliation, and peak metric compares
one-step $y_{t+1}$ with $\hat y_{t+1\mid t}$.
$H$ changes training-objective dimensionality and admissible origins near split
endpoints; it is not the evaluated forecast lead.
Approximate joint target spans are about $42.285$\,s ($H{=}1$), $5.64$\,min
($H{=}8$), and $11.28$\,min ($H{=}16$).
Scored timestamp sets need not match across $H$ because larger widths remove
final origins---especially on {CPU} fold~$0$, with smaller endpoint differences
for memory and disk (supplement).
Comparisons across $H$ are configuration-robustness checks, not controlled
lead-time comparisons.

\subsection{Model Selection and Leakage Prevention}
Each outer fold follows a nested sequence: construct the chronological split;
fit preprocessing on outer-training data only; tune on training/inner
validation; freeze one configuration per model family; execute under the frozen
implementation; evaluate once on the untouched outer-test set; and estimate
reconciliation without outer-test residuals.

Scaling is model-specific.
DLinear standardizes inputs and targets from training statistics only;
validation/test reuse those transforms, and predictions are inverse-transformed
before metrics.
Persistence, {EWMA}, Ridge, and LightGBM use the native scale (no shared
scaler).
Persistence has no selected hyperparameters.
{EWMA} $\alpha$ is selected on inner validation (frozen $\alpha{=}0.9$).
Ridge uses the shared-tuning grid ({CPU} $\alpha{=}10^{4}$; memory/disk
$\alpha{=}1$).
LightGBM is selected separately for {CPU}
($300$ trees, learning rate $0.03$, $31$ leaves) and memory
($100$, $0.05$, $15$); disk LightGBM reuses the memory configuration as stress
evidence only.
DLinear uses Adam/{MSE} with the frozen budget ($\le$40 epochs, patience~$5$,
one thread, $\le$200 batches/epoch, $180$\,s timeout).
Selected values and full search spaces are in the supplement.

Bottom-up needs no covariance.
Top-down proportions use independent base forecasts under the frozen
epsilon/\allowbreak fallback policy.
{WLS} uses diagonal variances from outer-train validation residuals.
The frozen MinT shrink covariance uses the same residual source.
Outer-test residuals never enter reconciliation matrices; reconciliation acts on
aligned one-step base forecasts.
Safeguards are chronological splits, train-only scaling where used,
inner-validation-only tuning, no outer-label selection,
train/\allowbreak validation residual covariance only, train-derived peak
thresholds, and immutable configurations before claim-generating runs
(not a claim that every bias is removed).

\subsection{Statistical and Multi-Seed Analysis}
Deterministic comparisons preserve hierarchy, model, reconciliation method,
output width, and fold.
Stochastic robustness also preserves seed, fold, and output width; seed
replicates are not independent datasets.
Paired moving-block bootstrap with $5000$ repetitions resamples absolute-error
series on shared timestamps.
Block length is clamped to $[8,256]$ after taking the maximum of $H$, $L$, and
the first autocorrelation lag below $0.1$ on validation residuals of the
independent top series (often $32$ when context dominates).
Within each replicate the relative {MAE} effect
$(\mathrm{MAE}_A^\ast-\mathrm{MAE}_B^\ast)/\mathrm{MAE}_B^\ast$
is recomputed directly---not by dividing independent confidence intervals,
averaging precomputed per-cell percentages, or taking ratios of globally pooled
means.

Raw hypothesis-test probabilities are Holm-adjusted within predefined families
\cite{holm1979simple}.
The final robustness analysis uses eight families (supplement).
Claim classification combines relative-effect direction, the direct confidence
interval, fold consistency, output-width consistency, seed consistency where
applicable, and top/bottom trade-off evidence; a majority of wins is not enough
for robust support.
For memory, seed-mean pooling alone cannot upgrade a claim when a seed reverses
direction.

LightGBM and DLinear use seeds $0$, $1$, and $2$.
Frozen LightGBM predictions are bitwise identical across seeds and are reported
as invariance, not as three independent realizations.
DLinear retains seed-dependent variation, and seed-level results remain visible.
Performance effects are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Peak thresholds $q_{90}$ and $q_{95}$ come from training observations only and
transfer unchanged to evaluation data.
Predictions and observations are compared at identical timestamps with no
event-tolerance window or event merging.
Reported quantities include high-load {MAE}, precision, recall, {F1}, false
alarms per evaluation day, signed bias, and predicted range compression where
relevant.
This tests transfer from point accuracy to operational high-load behavior;
models were not optimized for peak metrics.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Frozen Evidence}
The study separates prediction generation, primary statistical analysis, peak
analysis, robustness extension, robustness statistics, and final reporting
aggregation.
Accepted predictions are not regenerated during claim analysis; source artifact
hashes are checked; provisional or rejected packs are excluded; final tables come
from an evidence registry; and analysis/reporting freezes are immutable.
The dataset fingerprint is fixed, no model is rerun during final aggregation, and
claim-generating tables trace to accepted artifacts.
Exact freeze tags, peeled commits, pack hashes, and inventories are in the
supplement and reproducibility package.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Reconciliation Helps {CPU} Forecasting}
\label{sec:disc-cpu}
The independent {CPU} ranking reflects baseline difficulty rather than a
universal model preference.
Persistence and {EWMA} test recent levels; Ridge adds regularized linear lags;
DLinear tests a lightweight neural decomposition; LightGBM probes nonlinear
lag interactions \cite{ke2017lightgbm,zeng2023dlinear}.
LightGBM's advantage is specific to core-weighted {CPU}, the TimeTrack
post-outage timeline, one-step evaluation, and the frozen protocol---not a claim
that gradient boosting dominates cloud telemetry forecasting in general.

The positive reconciliation result is consistent with complementary forecast
error across hierarchy levels
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,wickramasuriya2019mint,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical}.
Independent top and bottom models estimate related physical quantities whose
errors need not share direction or scale.
A covariance-aware projection can combine these imperfect forecasts while
enforcing the summing relation; MinT uses estimated residual relationships rather
than replacing one level with another.
The observed gains suggest useful cross-level residual structure in the
evaluated trace, without causally identifying a specific workload mechanism.

Bottom-up and {WLS}/MinT serve different operational goals.
Bottom-up keeps machine forecasts unchanged and revises only the aggregate,
which helps when machine actions must stay fixed, but bottom errors then flow
into the cluster estimate.
{WLS}/MinT revise both levels and can raise aggregate accuracy more, sometimes
at machine-level cost (Figure~\ref{fig:trade}).
In the frozen outer evaluation, Ridge with bottom-up is the bottom-preserving
alternative and LightGBM with MinT the best observed aggregate configuration;
neither is universally preferred.
Exact coherence is necessary for physically consistent planning but insufficient
for accuracy: coherent yet inaccurate forecasts can still support poor decisions,
while accurate yet incoherent forecasts create contradictory capacity signals.
The useful outcome jointly achieves acceptable top accuracy, acceptable bottom
accuracy, and exact physical consistency.

\subsection{Hierarchy-Dependent Method Selection}
\label{sec:disc-select}
Memory shows why a strong independent baseline can dominate learned alternatives
even when reconciliation helps a model relative to itself.
The evaluated series are sufficiently level-persistent for {EWMA} to remain the
strongest robust comparator, so recent-level smoothing can beat more complex
learners on these traces.
Learned models may still improve relative to their own incoherent forecasts
without beating that baseline.
Seed-aware evidence is essential: the seed-$2$ {WLS}-versus-{EWMA} reversal
shows that pooled means can conceal direction changes.
This interpretation concerns the observed TimeTrack memory traces rather than a
claim that memory is universally smooth.

Disk is a boundary because used-disk level is additive and can exhibit
cleanup-, rotation-, or reset-like downward changes that make bottoms less
suitable as aggregate replacements.
Bottom-up trusts bottoms and worsens the aggregate when bottom errors accumulate;
top-down protects the independent top while transferring cost to machines;
{WLS}/MinT do not reproduce the {CPU} pattern.
Individual reset events were not separately labeled or causally tested.

The frozen evidence therefore supports hierarchy-aware selection rather than a
single named algorithm.
For {CPU} aggregate planning, LightGBM with MinT is the best observed
configuration; for machine-preserving {CPU} operation, Ridge with bottom-up
remains defensible.
For memory, {EWMA} independent is preferred; reconciliation is useful mainly to
improve a specific learned model relative to itself.
For disk aggregates, persistence/{EWMA} remain practical baselines and bottom-up
should not be default; top-down preserves the independent top only with
bottom-level cost.
This interprets the frozen outer evaluation; it is not a prospectively validated
production policy.
Compared with studies focused mainly on independent accuracy
\cite{masdari2020workload,hyndman2021forecasting}, the evaluation shows how
physical consistency, bottom cost, seed variation, and peak behavior alter method
choice \cite{athanasopoulos2024hierarchical}.
Physical exactness of an observed hierarchy does not guarantee forecast benefit
from every coherent projection.

\subsection{Implications for Application Profiling and Operation}
\label{sec:disc-ops}
A predictive application profile could store expected demand, forecast timestamp
and validity interval, hierarchy level, reconciliation method, coherence status,
uncertainty metadata, top/bottom adjustment magnitude, and model/data provenance.
This paper evaluates the forecast layer needed to populate such fields; it does
not modify a profile schema or claim an evaluated orchestrator integration.

Incoherent predictive fields could report one cluster requirement from machine
forecasts and another from the aggregate, risking inconsistent placement,
duplicated reservation, mis-allocation, contradictory scaling, and weak
post-hoc explanation.
These risks remain conceptual: no live orchestration experiment was conducted
\cite{meliani2026zerotouch}.
A safe pipeline suggested by the evidence would maintain hierarchy metadata;
evaluate strong independent baselines; fit reconciliation only from
train/validation residuals; compare top and bottom accuracy separately; retain a
no-reconciliation option; validate peaks independently from ordinary {MAE}; and
record method trade-offs in provenance---as methodology, not deployed software.
Ordinary {MAE} and peak behavior diverge because averages dilute rare high-load
regions and models may reduce common-state error while compressing extremes.
The DLinear memory compression diagnostic shows that reconciliation can restore
sums without restoring dynamic range; recall and false alarms depend on threshold
crossings, not only error magnitude
(Table~\ref{tab:stats}; supplementary peak panels).

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}
\label{sec:disc-limits}
\paragraph{Limitations.}
Evidence is limited to one public TimeTrack dataset from a seven-machine {OAI}
{CI}/{CD} environment and one retained post-outage segment
\cite{meliani2025timetrack,meliani2025timetrackkaggle}; multi-site replication is
required before broader claims.
All final metrics are one-step-ahead: $H$ is joint training output width, not
evaluated lead time, and scored timestamp sets differ slightly across $H$, so
medium-/long-lead performance is not established.
Model scope is finite (selected classical, tree, and lightweight neural
families; transferred disk LightGBM as stress only; three DLinear seeds;
LightGBM invariance under deterministic settings; observational rather than
prospectively selected winners).
Peak analysis uses train-derived $q_{90}/q_{95}$ thresholds with exact-timestamp
matching and no event tolerance; no live autoscaling/{SLA}/energy loop was run;
bootstrap evidence is timeline-conditional; probabilistic calibration,
network-hierarchy claims, and valid temporal-resolution studies remain out of
scope.
An unavailable downsampling helper is excluded rather than treated as a negative
empirical finding.

\paragraph{Future research.}
Priorities include true lead-time evaluation separating lead $\ell$ from output
width $H$ on common timestamps; multi-dataset and dynamic-hierarchy validation;
adaptive independent/bottom-up/{WLS}/MinT/top-down selection with prospective
checks; probabilistic coherent intervals for uncertainty-aware profiles;
peak-aware objectives and event-oriented evaluation; profile/orchestration
integration with downstream {SLA}/energy outcomes; and valid
temporal-resolution studies based on real resampling.
These identify open research, not completed freeze contributions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Machine- and cluster-level telemetry forecasts can be individually accurate yet
physically inconsistent, undermining their use in application profiling,
planning, and orchestration.
This study evaluated hierarchy-aware forecasting and reconciliation on
TimeTrack's verified machine-to-cluster relations for core-weighted {CPU},
memory, and disk, comparing persistence, {EWMA}, Ridge, LightGBM, and DLinear
in independent and reconciled modes (bottom-up, {WLS}, MinT-shrink, and
top-down for the disk boundary).

On core-weighted {CPU} under the frozen one-step outer evaluation, LightGBM is
the strongest observed independent model, improving on Ridge by about $17.3\%$.
LightGBM MinT yields a further approximately $10.1\%$ aggregate {MAE} reduction
relative to LightGBM independent while restoring exact machine-to-cluster
coherence.
LightGBM is seed-invariant under the frozen deterministic configuration, and
DLinear {CPU} reconciliation effects are practically seed-stable.
These conclusions are scoped to TimeTrack, core-weighted {CPU}, one-step
scoring of the first multi-output component, and the frozen protocol; they are
not a claim of universal superiority.
The {CPU} pattern persists across outer folds, multi-output-width
configurations, models, and seeds, but is not established across evaluated lead
times.

Memory and disk establish hierarchy-dependent selection rather than a single
reconciliation strategy.
For memory, reconciliation often improves DLinear relative to its own
independent forecast, yet {EWMA} remains the strongest robust comparator and
reconciled DLinear does not robustly outperform {EWMA} across seeds.
For disk, exact hierarchy validity does not guarantee reconciliation gains:
Ridge bottom-up harms aggregate accuracy, whereas top-down preserves the
independent top forecast at a machine-level cost.

Ordinary {MAE} improvements do not imply universal high-load or peak-detection
gains.
LightGBM remains the strongest among the evaluated {CPU} models during
high-load periods, but general reconciliation benefits for recall and
false-alarm behavior are not established.
DLinear memory underprediction and range compression persist across seeds and
can be amplified by reconciliation.
Exact coherence, average predictive accuracy, bottom-level accuracy, and
peak-operational behavior must therefore be assessed separately.

Relative to earlier unified application-profile work, the present study
supplies an evidence-backed coherent forecasting layer that could later support
dynamic profile fields such as predicted demand, hierarchy level, coherence
status, reconciliation provenance, and uncertainty metadata.
No complete application-profile integration and no live orchestration or
autoscaling loop were evaluated.
The practical lesson is to retain strong independent baselines, explicit
hierarchy metadata, a no-reconciliation option, and separate top/bottom
trade-off reporting.

All final metrics are one-step-ahead: $H$ is joint training output width rather
than evaluated lead time, and the evidence comes from one dataset and one
retained timeline, so no medium- or long-lead conclusion is supported.
Priority next steps are true multi-lead evaluation with a separate lead
variable; replication across datasets and changing hierarchies; prospective
adaptive method selection; probabilistic coherent uncertainty estimates; and
integration into profile maintenance and orchestration decisions.
Coherent telemetry forecasting should therefore be treated as a selective
decision layer whose value depends jointly on hierarchy semantics, baseline
strength, forecasting objective, and operational cost.
