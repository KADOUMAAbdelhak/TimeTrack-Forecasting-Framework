\begin{abstract}
Independent machine-level forecasts of cloud telemetry often violate exact
machine-to-cluster conservation, producing incoherent capacity views even when
point accuracy appears acceptable. Using the public TimeTrack OpenAirInterface
({OAI}) continuous integration/continuous deployment telemetry dataset, we
evaluate hierarchy-aware forecasting on verified physical hierarchies for
core-weighted {CPU}, exact memory aggregation, and disk-level summing.
Baselines include persistence, exponentially weighted moving average ({EWMA}),
and Ridge regression; learned models include LightGBM and DLinear; reconciliation
modes include independent forecasts, bottom-up, weighted least squares ({WLS}),
and MinT-shrink, with top-down used for the disk boundary.
On {CPU}, LightGBM with MinT yields the best observed frozen outer-evaluation
accuracy while restoring exact coherence.
On memory, reconciliation often improves DLinear relative to its own independent
forecast, yet {EWMA} remains the strongest observed method.
On disk, Ridge bottom-up degrades aggregate accuracy, whereas top-down preserves
the independent top forecast at a bottom-level cost.
Ordinary aggregate mean absolute error ({MAE}) gains do not transfer to universal
peak-operational improvements.
These results support selective, hierarchy-dependent reconciliation as a practical
layer for coherent multi-level telemetry forecasting in application-profiling
pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Coherent machine-to-cluster forecasts via hierarchy-aware reconciliation.
\item LightGBM+MinT improves {CPU} accuracy and restores exact coherence.
\item Memory gains are conditional; {EWMA} remains the strongest observed method.
\item Disk shows a failure boundary: bottom-up can harm aggregate accuracy.
\item Ordinary {MAE} gains do not imply universal peak-operational improvements.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Cloud--edge and cloud-native platforms generate telemetry at several physical
and administrative levels at once: containers or workloads, machines or nodes,
clusters, and, in continuum settings, higher-level orchestration views
\cite{buyya2019manifesto,shi2016edge,satyanarayanan2017emergence}.
Capacity allocation, proactive scaling, placement, admission control,
service-level agreement ({SLA}) risk assessment, and ongoing profile maintenance
increasingly depend on forecasts of those signals, not only on instantaneous
measurements
\cite{lorido2014review,qu2018auto}.
When decisions consume both machine-level and cluster-level forecasts, the
forecasts must describe the same underlying system rather than two
independently convenient summaries of related but unconstrained streams.

Independently accurate forecasts can still fail in operation.
Consider seven machine {CPU} forecasts whose core-weighted combination implies
one cluster total, while a separately trained cluster forecast implies another.
Each series may achieve a competitive mean absolute error ({MAE}), yet the two
totals cannot simultaneously represent the same physical hierarchy.
The resulting inconsistency can produce contradictory capacity estimates,
incoherent application profiles, unstable or infeasible orchestration actions,
and difficulty explaining forecast-driven decisions, including double counting
or underestimating available resources.
In short, point-wise competitiveness does not guarantee a usable joint plan.
Forecast coherence---exact consistency with verified machine-to-cluster
aggregation---is therefore an operational requirement for multi-level planning,
not merely a statistical preference
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical}.

Conventional cloud workload and resource forecasting largely optimizes point
accuracy on monitored streams
\cite{calheiros2015workload,masdari2020workload,khan2022workload}.
Autoscaling and proactive resource-management studies likewise often treat host
and aggregate telemetry as independent inputs to control loops
\cite{toka2021kubernetes,casalicchio2019container}.
Model comparisons typically ask which predictor minimizes error, whereas fewer
evaluations ask whether forecasts respect exact multi-level identities that
link machines to clusters.
Selecting the lowest-{MAE} model therefore does not automatically resolve
machine-to-cluster inconsistency: an accurate independent bottom forecast and
an accurate independent top forecast may still disagree about the same physical
quantity.

This paper continues a line of application-profiling research in the
cloud--edge continuum.
Our earlier unified application-profile model for microservices-based
applications formalized requirements, components, deployment constraints, and
continuum resources in a single representation, and identified predictive
resource behavior as a direction for moving from descriptive profiles toward
proactive profile management
\cite{kadouma2025unifiedprofile}.
The present work develops and evaluates the coherent forecasting and
reconciliation layer needed for that progression.
It does not redesign the profile ontology or schema; instead, it supplies
freeze-scoped forecast evidence that can later populate or maintain dynamic
application-profile fields under exact machine-to-cluster constraints.

We study the problem on TimeTrack, a public OpenAirInterface ({OAI}) continuous
integration/continuous deployment ({CI}/{CD}) telemetry dataset with seven
machines, cluster-level aggregates, and multiple resource metrics
\cite{meliani2025timetrack,meliani2025timetrackkaggle}.
In our pipeline the measured median sampling interval is approximately
$42.285$\,s.
The hierarchies used here have exact or otherwise verified aggregation
relations and exhibit operationally realistic {CI}/{CD} workload variation.
We distinguish roles carefully: the TimeTrack dataset paper presents and
characterizes the corpus; the Kaggle release provides public access; Zero-Touch
Predictive Orchestration studies automated model generation and cold-start
mitigation on related continuum forecasting problems
\cite{meliani2026zerotouch};
and the present paper evaluates hierarchy-aware coherence, reconciliation
trade-offs, stochastic robustness, and failure boundaries on frozen TimeTrack
artifacts.
Dataset creation is not claimed as a contribution here.

The research gap is the lack of an evaluation that jointly combines verified
physical machine-to-cluster telemetry hierarchies; strong deterministic and
learned forecasting baselines; independent forecasts together with multiple
reconciliation strategies; leakage-controlled chronological outer evaluation;
moving-block bootstrap with multiple-comparison correction; stochastic-seed
robustness; top-versus-bottom accuracy trade-offs; peak-operational
qualification; and both positive and negative hierarchy cases.
General hierarchical forecasting and reconciliation methods exist
\cite{wickramasuriya2019mint,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical},
and related cloud hierarchical learning appears in settings such as
end-to-end multi-indicator models
\cite{harmony2024},
yet their behavior is not automatically transferable to cloud telemetry.
Hierarchy semantics differ by metric; top and bottom predictability differ;
disk levels can include resets or large downward movements; reconciliation can
propagate poor bottom forecasts into aggregates; and operational peak metrics
may respond differently from ordinary average error.
An evidence-based account of when reconciliation helps, when it is conditional,
and when it fails is therefore required before coherent forecasts can be treated
as a default planning layer.

Our study therefore compares persistence, exponentially weighted moving average
({EWMA}), Ridge regression, LightGBM
\cite{ke2017lightgbm},
and DLinear
\cite{zeng2023dlinear}
under independent, bottom-up, weighted least squares ({WLS}), and MinT-shrink
modes, with top-down reserved for the disk boundary.
Hierarchies comprise verified core-weighted {CPU}, an exact memory sum, and an
exact disk-level sum.
Evaluation uses chronological outer folds, multiple horizons, train-only
hyperparameter selection, seeds $0/1/2$ for LightGBM and DLinear, and immutable
frozen evidence layers culminating in the reporting freeze.
Detailed formulations and protocol parameters are given in
Sections~\ref{sec:methods} and~\ref{sec:protocol}; the Introduction states only
the design commitments needed to interpret the research questions and claims.

In the frozen outer evaluation, LightGBM independent outperforms Ridge by about
$17.3\%$ on core-weighted {CPU}, and LightGBM MinT further reduces aggregate
{CPU} {MAE} by about $10.1\%$ relative to LightGBM independent while restoring
exact coherence; LightGBM is seed-invariant under the frozen configuration, and
DLinear reconciliation gains remain stable across seeds.
On memory, reconciliation often improves DLinear relative to its own independent
forecast, yet {EWMA} remains the strongest robust method and reconciled DLinear
does not robustly outperform {EWMA}.
On disk, Ridge bottom-up harms aggregate accuracy, whereas top-down preserves
the independent top forecast at a bottom-level cost.
Across peak and high-load diagnostics, ordinary aggregate-{MAE} gains do not
imply universal peak-recall or false-alarm improvements.
These findings are scoped to the frozen TimeTrack evaluation and are presented
as observed outer-evaluation outcomes rather than as a prospectively selected
deployment policy.

These observations motivate five research questions.
\textbf{RQ1:} How do independent and reconciled forecasts affect aggregate
accuracy and exact machine-to-cluster coherence for core-weighted {CPU}?
\textbf{RQ2:} Do the {CPU} findings remain stable across folds, horizons,
models, and stochastic seeds?
\textbf{RQ3:} When does reconciliation improve memory forecasts, and how do
reconciled learned models compare with {EWMA}?
\textbf{RQ4:} Why can reconciliation degrade disk-level forecasts, and what
trade-offs arise between bottom-up and top-down modes?
\textbf{RQ5:} Do ordinary aggregate-accuracy gains transfer to high-load and
peak-operational metrics?

Using only frozen final evidence, this paper contributes:
(i)~a hierarchy-aware forecasting formulation for verified machine-to-cluster
{CPU}, memory, and disk relations, enabling exact coherence assessment rather
than independent stream scoring alone;
(ii)~a leakage-controlled comparison of persistence, {EWMA}, Ridge, LightGBM,
and DLinear under independent, bottom-up, {WLS}, and MinT-shrink modes, with
top-down for disk, quantifying accuracy--coherence and top/bottom trade-offs;
(iii)~seed-robust {CPU} evidence that LightGBM+MinT is the best observed frozen
outer-evaluation configuration for aggregate accuracy with restored coherence;
(iv)~conditional memory evidence that reconciled DLinear can improve over its
independent forecast while {EWMA} remains the strongest robust method;
(v)~a disk failure boundary in which Ridge bottom-up harms aggregates and
top-down preserves the independent top at bottom cost; and
(vi)~peak-operational qualification showing that ordinary aggregate-{MAE} gains
do not imply universal high-load or peak improvements.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work;
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes TimeTrack and the hierarchies;
Sections~\ref{sec:methods}--\ref{sec:protocol} present methodology and protocol;
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations; and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Application Profiling in the Cloud--Edge Continuum}
Application-profile and lifecycle-management research in the cloud--edge
continuum seeks declarative descriptions that make placement, adaptation, and
operations tractable across heterogeneous tiers
\cite{duc2020survey,taherizadeh2018monitoring,buyya2019manifesto}.
Typical profiles encode components and dependencies, resource requirements,
deployment constraints, placement preferences, service objectives, and lifecycle
hooks that orchestration platforms can consume.
Container orchestration surveys likewise emphasize monitoring and control loops
that operate on multi-granularity telemetry rather than on a single aggregate
view
\cite{casalicchio2019container,rossi2020geo,toka2021kubernetes}.
As continuum applications span edge and cloud nodes, those loops increasingly
need forecasts that remain meaningful when read jointly at machine and cluster
scopes.

Telemetry-aware and adaptive profiling extends these representations by feeding
current measurements---and, increasingly, predicted resource behavior---into
placement and scaling decisions
\cite{taherizadeh2018monitoring,duc2020survey}.
Consistency becomes harder when predictions are produced independently at
machine and cluster levels: a profile that stores both views can encode
contradictory capacity expectations even when each forecast appears accurate in
isolation.
In contrast to representation-centric profile work, forecast-oriented cloud
management literature typically optimizes prediction error or scaling cost
without asking whether multi-level profile fields remain jointly coherent.

Our earlier unified application-profile model for microservices in the continuum
formalized requirements, components, constraints, and resources in one schema
and identified predictive resource behavior as a step toward proactive profile
maintenance
\cite{kadouma2025unifiedprofile}.
The present paper supplies a forecasting and reconciliation evaluation that may
support such future maintenance under exact machine-to-cluster identities.
It evaluates coherent telemetry forecasts; it does not propose another
application-profile schema or claim an end-to-end profile-management system.

\subsection{Cloud Telemetry and Workload Forecasting}
Classical cloud resource forecasting established persistence, smoothing, and
autoregressive baselines as practical references for provisioning and quality of
service
\cite{calheiros2015workload,islam2012empirical}.
Persistence and exponentially weighted moving average ({EWMA}) remain important
not because they are sophisticated, but because they expose whether learned
models improve upon stable, train-only-tuned alternatives under the same
protocol.
Without such references, apparent gains from tree ensembles or neural forecasters
can be overstated relative to inexpensive, reproducible baselines that operators
already understand.
Surveys of workload and resource forecasting therefore treat strong baselines as
part of evaluation hygiene rather than as obsolete methods
\cite{masdari2020workload,lorido2014review}.

Machine-learning approaches, including regression, tree ensembles such as
LightGBM
\cite{ke2017lightgbm},
and neural models, are widely applied to {CPU}, memory, request rates, and
related autoscaling signals
\cite{khan2022workload,qu2018auto,toka2021kubernetes}.
Their common evaluation practice optimizes point accuracy on independently
monitored streams and then, in operational settings, feeds those forecasts into
reactive or proactive controllers.
This line of work addresses model selection and control policy design rather
than hierarchy consistency: host and cluster signals may both be predicted, yet
nothing enforces that the predictions describe one physical system.

Deep forecasting surveys document rapid progress with recurrent, attention, and
Transformer architectures
\cite{lim2021temporal,wen2023transformers}.
Comparative evidence nevertheless cautions that complex models are not
automatically better across datasets and metrics
\cite{makridakis2018statistical,zeng2023dlinear}.
DLinear is particularly relevant here as a deliberately simple neural baseline
that can match or exceed more elaborate Transformers on several forecasting
benchmarks
\cite{zeng2023dlinear}.
Using LightGBM and DLinear in our study therefore tests competitive learned
forecasters; it does not claim that the models themselves are novel.

Cloud-native and edge platforms consume node and cluster telemetry for
Kubernetes scaling, geographically distributed deployment, and continuum
orchestration
\cite{toka2021kubernetes,rossi2020geo,casalicchio2019container}.
Proactive autoscaling taxonomies stress bursty demand and the cost of delayed
reactions
\cite{lorido2014review,qu2018auto},
yet controllers typically treat monitored streams as independent inputs.
Signature-based and anomaly-aware predictors further show that average error can
hide costly deviations in resource usage
\cite{sus2024signature}.
Spike-sensitive adjustments for cloud provisioning likewise argue that ordinary
forecast scores understate sudden increases that drive under-provisioning
\cite{agullo2025spikes}.
These perspectives motivate peak and high-load qualification ({RQ5}) without
turning this manuscript into a full autoscaling or {SLA}-control study.

Public multi-metric telecommunications cloud-native traces remain comparatively
scarce.
TimeTrack provides {OAI} {CI}/{CD} telemetry with machine and cluster views
\cite{meliani2025timetrack,meliani2025timetrackkaggle}.
The dataset paper characterizes the corpus; the Kaggle release enables public
reuse; Zero-Touch Predictive Orchestration studies automated model generation
and cold-start mitigation on related continuum forecasting problems
\cite{meliani2026zerotouch}.
Unlike those contributions, the present study does not automate model search or
reintroduce the dataset.
It instead evaluates machine-to-cluster coherence under classical bottom-up,
{WLS}, and MinT selectivity, including disk failure boundaries, multi-seed
robustness, and peak qualification on frozen TimeTrack hierarchies.

\subsection{Hierarchical Forecasting and Reconciliation}
Independently forecast nodes of a hierarchy generally violate aggregation
constraints: base forecasts need not coincide with coherent forecasts that
respect a summing matrix linking bottom series to aggregates
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,hyndman2021forecasting}.
The forecasting literature therefore separates the production of base forecasts
from their projection onto the constraint space, treating coherence as a
structural requirement rather than as an optional post-check.
We leave formal matrix notation to Section~\ref{sec:methods} and focus here on
method classes and their empirical assumptions.

Classical strategies include bottom-up aggregation of bottom forecasts,
top-down disaggregation of a top forecast, ordinary least-squares combination,
and weighted least squares ({WLS})
\cite{hyndman2011optimal,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical}.
Bottom-up preserves bottoms but can inject bottom-level error into the aggregate;
top-down preserves the top while redistributing residuals to bottoms; combination
methods adjust all levels using estimated error structure.
The practical choice among these modes therefore depends on where forecast error
is concentrated and on whether the operational objective privileges top accuracy,
bottom fidelity, or joint coherence.
Where bottom predictability is weak, bottom-up can therefore harm aggregates---a
failure mode that is especially relevant for disk-like telemetry with resets or
large downward movements.

MinT reconciles base forecasts by minimizing the trace of the reconciled error
covariance under the summing constraints, often with shrinkage estimators that
stabilize covariance estimates in moderate samples
\cite{wickramasuriya2019mint,schafer2005shrinkage}.
Geometric interpretations clarify bias and projection properties of
reconciliation
\cite{panagiotelis2021geometric},
while linear-model formulations show that forecasting and reconciliation can be
combined efficiently when base models are linear
\cite{ashouri2022fast}.
Recent reviews synthesize these developments across point and probabilistic
settings
\cite{athanasopoulos2024hierarchical,panagiotelis2023probabilistic}.
The present paper reuses classical MinT-shrink/{WLS}/bottom-up/top-down
machinery; it does not introduce a new reconciliation algorithm.

Computing systems increasingly explore hierarchical or multi-level resource
prediction, but often under different hierarchy semantics.
{HARMONY} couples hierarchical multi-indicator attention forecasting with
{Bayesian} scaling decisions for cloud resources; its hierarchy is
indicator-structural and end-to-end rather than a post-hoc classical
reconciliation suite on exact machine-to-cluster sums
\cite{harmony2024}.
The Flow-based Reconcile Transformer ({FRT}) jointly forecasts and reconciles
hierarchical series with coherent deep generative modeling and reports
data-center workload deployments, again as an end-to-end neural method rather
than a selective classical ablation on physical {CPU}, memory, and disk
identities
\cite{wang2025frt}.
{MaMiClif} models macro-micro microservice indicator dependencies in logical
data centers through collaborative graph learning; the hierarchy is
service/indicator relational rather than an exact machine-to-cluster summing
relation
\cite{gao2026mamiclif}.
These systems are valuable adjacent baselines for hierarchical cloud prediction
and coherent deep forecasting.
Across them, however, a method that improves aggregate prediction for one
hierarchy definition need not transfer to core-weighted {CPU}, additive memory,
or disk levels whose bottom errors can propagate under bottom-up reconciliation.
That transfer question is empirical and hierarchy-dependent, which is precisely
the scoped evaluation gap addressed below.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Table~\ref{tab:related} situates the closest work.
Cloud forecasting usually evaluates independent streams; reconciliation research
often uses economic, energy, retail, tourism, or synthetic hierarchies; and
computing-oriented hierarchical studies may omit strong deterministic baselines,
seed robustness, or negative hierarchy cases
\cite{masdari2020workload,athanasopoulos2024hierarchical,harmony2024,wang2025frt}.
Average forecasting metrics also may not describe peak-operational behavior
relevant to provisioning risk
\cite{agullo2025spikes,sus2024signature}.
Few evaluated systems combine verified physical machine-to-cluster relations,
metric-specific hierarchy semantics, classical reconciliation selectivity, and
operational peak qualification on public {OAI} telemetry.

Relative to those closest studies, this work jointly examines verified
core-weighted {CPU}, exact memory, and exact disk hierarchies on TimeTrack.
It compares persistence, {EWMA}, Ridge, LightGBM, and DLinear under independent,
bottom-up, {WLS}, and MinT-shrink modes, with top-down reserved for the disk
boundary, and reports leakage-controlled outer folds, moving-block bootstrap
with Holm correction, seeds $0/1/2$, top/bottom trade-offs, peak qualification,
and a disk negative boundary.
That scoped combination addresses {RQ1}--{RQ5} without claiming a universal first
method or cross-dataset numerical superiority.

\section{TimeTrack Dataset and Telemetry Hierarchies}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Collection environment and trace structure.}
TimeTrack is a public high-resolution telemetry corpus collected from an
operational OpenAirInterface ({OAI}) continuous integration and deployment
({CI}/{CD}) cluster that supports development and testing of {OAI} components
such as the {gNB}, user equipment ({UE}), and core-network software
\cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
The published environment comprises seven physical machines with $236$ {CPU}
cores, $437.5$\,{GB} {RAM}, approximately $1.8$\,{TB} of {SSD} storage, and
multiple physical network interfaces; Prometheus is reported as the underlying
metric source \cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
Released traces cover computing, per-core {CPU}, disk, memory, network, and
latency signals and are available on {Kaggle}
\cite{meliani2025timetrackkaggle}.
Dataset creation is not a contribution of this manuscript: we take the released
files as given and begin with hierarchy validation and hierarchy-aware
forecasting on the metrics required for the accepted exact {CPU}, memory, and
disk relations summarized in Table~\ref{tab:hierarchies}.
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows independent bottom- and top-level forecasts under
exact summing constraints with optional reconciliation.

\paragraph{Timeline preparation and integrity checks.}
The dataset paper describes a nominal collection interval of approximately
$45$\,s \cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
Direct timestamp analysis yields a measured median interval of approximately
$42.285$\,s (about $99.93\%$ of consecutive steps round to $42$\,s), which we
use for horizon interpretation.
Five primary traces share $41{,}362$ timestamps from
$2024$-$06$-$24$~$13$:$37$:$06$ to $2024$-$07$-$19$~$16$:$27$:$05$
($\approx 25.12$ calendar days), with no duplicate timestamps and strictly
increasing order.
A major outage from $2024$-$06$-$28$~$13$:$10$:$49$ to
$2024$-$07$-$03$~$10$:$05$:$20$ ($\approx 4.87$ days) is not interpolated:
multi-day fill would invent temporal structure.
Forecasting windows use only the retained continuous post-outage segment
($33{,}235$ observations); no context window may cross the outage.
Exact fold boundaries are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
Sparse missing cells outside the outage are left missing rather than filled
across the break; hierarchy identity checks use timestamp-aligned rows without
imputing invented values.

\paragraph{Selected metrics.}
We use per-machine {CPU} consumption percentages with a core-weighted cluster
representation; used-memory levels (bytes) at machine and cluster scope; and
used-disk levels ({UD}, bytes) at both scopes.
{UD} denotes a used-disk level, not disk write rate, disk read rate, or
cumulative {I}/{O} counters.
Network-interface and latency telemetry are present in TimeTrack but are not
used for a final network-hierarchy claim: candidate bond/interface aggregation
did not meet the same exact physical-identity bar as {CPU}, memory, and disk.

\paragraph{Core-weighted {CPU} hierarchy.}
Let $u_{i,t}$ be machine~$i$'s {CPU}-utilization percentage and $c_i$ its
verified core count, with $C=\sum_i c_i=236$.
Reported cluster {CPU} is the weighted mean $(\sum_i c_i u_{i,t})/C$.
Reconciliation uses the additive weighted-sum hierarchy
$y^{\mathrm{CPU}}_t=\sum_i c_i u_{i,t}$, converting to percentage only for
reporting as $y^{\mathrm{CPU}}_t/236$.
An unweighted mean would give equal weight to machines with unequal capacity.
Verified counts are
$(c_i)=(36,48,36,36,20,36,24)$ for machines~$01$--$07$ (total~$236$).
The TimeTrack resource table and raw static core-count fields swap the unequal
machine05/machine07 labels relative to the released per-core host identifiers
\cite{meliani2025timetrack}.
We resolve this by data-derived validation---correlating machine-level {CU}
with per-core host traces, comparing candidate mappings to host column
inventories, and retaining the mapping that yields an exact weighted-sum
identity with $C=236$---not by arbitrary relabeling.
The total remains $236$, but the two unequal machine-specific labels are
exchanged between the publication table and the telemetry identifiers; we use
the empirically verified mapping throughout forecasting and reconciliation.

\paragraph{Memory and disk hierarchies.}
Used memory obeys an exact byte-sum identity: the cluster used-memory series
equals the sum of the seven machine used-memory series.
Bytes are already additive, so no capacity weighting is needed.
On all $41{,}362$ timestamp-aligned compute rows the maximum absolute identity
error is~$0$ (no imputation for this check).
Independent forecasts can still violate the identity; reconciliation restores
it on forecasts.
Used disk obeys the analogous exact level-sum identity on the same
$41{,}362$ aligned rows, again with maximum absolute identity error~$0$.
{UD} is a level series: downward jumps may reflect cleanup, deletion, rotation,
or reset behavior, unlike smoother capacity-like dynamics.
Exact structural coherence does not imply that every reconciliation method
improves accuracy when bottoms respond differently to downward changes;
measured trade-offs are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Scope of the final hierarchy study.}
Before forecasting we verified numerically that core-weighted {CPU} is exact
under the verified mapping, that used-memory and used-disk sums are exact on
aligned observations, and that summing matrices encode these physical relations
for later reconciliation of forecasts.
Approximate network bond/interface candidates were inspected and excluded from
final claims.
This manuscript's contribution therefore begins after dataset release, with
validated hierarchies and the frozen protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\section{Forecasting and Reconciliation Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Forecasting Problem}
For each hierarchy, let bottom series $\mathbf{b}_t$ and top series $y_t$ satisfy
$y_t=\mathbf{s}^\top\mathbf{b}_t$ (or the core-weighted {CPU} analogue).
Given context windows ending at time $t$, a model produces horizon-$h$ forecasts
$\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{t+h}$ and $\hat{y}_{t+h}$.
Independent forecasts need not satisfy the summing constraint; reconciled
forecasts $\tilde{\mathbf{b}},\tilde{y}$ do.

\subsection{Baselines and Forecasting Models}
\label{sec:baselines}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} defines the baseline set used before any result
claim.
Persistence is a last-value forecast.
{EWMA} selects its smoothing parameter on inner validation only.
Ridge is the strongest deterministic learned {CPU} baseline in the frozen
evidence.
LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} is the strongest observed {CPU} model and is
seed-invariant under the frozen configuration for seeds $0/1/2$.
DLinear \cite{zeng2023dlinear} provides a neural baseline with nonzero but
generally small {CPU} seed variability.
{EWMA} is the strongest observed memory method.

\input{tables/baseline_definitions}

\subsection{Reconciliation Methods}
Bottom-up aggregates bottom forecasts through the summing matrix and preserves
bottoms.
{WLS} and MinT-shrink adjust base forecasts using variance/covariance structure;
MinT uses shrinkage covariance estimation
\cite{wickramasuriya2019mint,schafer2005shrinkage}.
Top-down is evaluated for disk to preserve an independently forecast top while
distributing to bottoms.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Primary accuracy uses {MAE}, with supporting {RMSE}, {MASE}, and $R^2$ where
available \cite{hyndman2006accuracy}.
Coherence error measures absolute violation of the exact summing relation before
and after reconciliation.
Peak and high-load metrics use train-derived thresholds from the frozen peak
analysis; they qualify rather than replace aggregate {MAE}.

\subsection{{CPU} Forecasting and Reconciliation}
\label{sec:cpu}
Table~\ref{tab:cpu_main} and Figure~\ref{fig:cpu_h} summarize {CPU} results in

Horizon-averaged independent {MAE} values are about $1.321$ (persistence),

\subsection{Memory Results}
\label{sec:memory}
Table~\ref{tab:memory_main} shows that {EWMA} is the strongest observed memory
method ({MAE} $\approx 1.051\times 10^9$).
DLinear independent is weaker than {EWMA}; {WLS}/MinT often improve DLinear
relative to its own independent forecast (about $-3.22\%$ / $-3.40\%$
seed-mean), but reconciled DLinear does not robustly beat {EWMA}.
The seed-mean {WLS}-versus-{EWMA} effect is near zero/slightly positive, and
seed~$2$ reverses ($\approx +2.06\%$), contradicting a robust win claim.
MinT versus {EWMA} remains unsupported as a robust improvement.
LightGBM is substantially worse than {EWMA}: the ratio of horizon-mean {MAE}s is
about $+36.9\%$, whereas the mean of horizon-level relative {MAE} fields is about
$+38.8\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:memory_main}); these are different aggregations, not
conflicting measurements of one scalar.
Figure~\ref{fig:mem_ewma} shows seed-wise relative effects versus {EWMA} for
DLinear and LightGBM.
Memory evidence is therefore secondary and conditional.

\subsection{Disk Failure Boundary}
\label{sec:disk}

LightGBM disk results in the freeze stack are transferred-configuration stress

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Accuracy--Coherence Trade-offs}
{RQ1}--{RQ2}: For core-weighted {CPU}, learned independent forecasts already beat
strong deterministic baselines, and MinT further improves LightGBM aggregates
while restoring exact coherence.
Bottom-up preserves bottoms; {WLS} and MinT expose an explicit top/bottom
trade-off.

\subsection{Why Reconciliation Is Hierarchy-Dependent}
{RQ3}--{RQ4}: Memory and disk demonstrate that reconciliation is not universally
beneficial.
On memory, {EWMA} can dominate learned models, so reconciliation should be judged
against {EWMA}, not only against a model's independent forecast.
On disk, bottom-up can inject bottom errors into the aggregate, while top-down
protects the top at bottom cost.

\subsection{Implications for Application Profiling}
For proactive profile management
\cite{kadouma2025unifiedprofile}, coherent {CPU} forecasts provide a practical
planning layer.
Memory and disk require selective policies: retain strong simple baselines when
they win, and choose reconciliation mode by hierarchy rather than by default.

\subsection{Limitations}
Evidence is single-dataset and freeze-scoped.
Sampling differs slightly from the approximate $45$\,s description in some
dataset materials; we report the measured median $42.285$\,s.
Network, downsampling, conformal, {LSTM}, adaptive routing, and {LOMO} results
are out of scope.
Public code packaging for this article may still require an author-confirmed
repository {URL}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Hierarchy-aware reconciliation yields strong and seed-robust {CPU} coherence and
accuracy gains on TimeTrack, conditional memory improvements that do not
displace {EWMA}, a clear disk failure boundary, and peak-operational limits on
how ordinary {MAE} gains transfer.
Selective reconciliation is therefore a practical forecasting layer for
multi-level cloud telemetry in application-profiling pipelines.
